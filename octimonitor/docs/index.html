The path of a number

Every value on the dashboard is measured on a real person's phone during normal use. Nothing is simulated or sampled in a lab.

  1. Measure. On every frame the app records how long the frame took, how long the CPU spent preparing it and how long the GPU spent drawing it. Each frame is tagged with the app's current scene: a material, a level, a tool, whatever the app chooses.
  2. Summarize. Every 30 seconds, and whenever the app goes to the background, the frames of that window are turned into a summary per scene: percentiles, frame rate, dropped frames, hitch time, plus memory and heat.
  3. Send. The summary goes out as one small JSON row to a Postgres table in Supabase. The app's key can only add rows, never read them.
  4. Aggregate. The dashboard calls one read-only database function every 10 seconds. It combines all rows in the selected range and returns totals and averages, never individual rows.

So a frame drawn right now shows up on the dashboard within about 40 seconds: up to 30 seconds until its window closes, plus up to 10 seconds until the next refresh.

Scenes

OctiMonitor is not tied to one app or one kind of content. Every frame carries a scene: a short name the app chooses for what is on screen. All per-part numbers on the dashboard, such as GPU cost, the scene × device grid and the inspector, are split by it.

AppScene meansExample names
Opal CaressThe material being played withSlime, Spark, Lightning
A sculpting appThe active toolSculpt, Smooth, Paint
A gameThe level or screenMenu, Level 3, Boss fight

Several apps can report into the same backend. Each row carries the app's bundle id, so the dashboard can show all apps together or one at a time, each with its own scenes, phones and builds.

Why the app picks the name. Only the app knows which parts of it cost differently to draw. A fixed list would fit one app and be meaningless for the next. Use a small, stable set of names: they become the rows of every breakdown, and at most 32 are kept apart.

What the app measures

OctiMonitor is a small Swift package for any iOS app that draws with Metal. The app calls it in two places in its renderer; everything else happens inside the package. The example below is how Opal Caress does it.

// In the command buffer's completed handler: the GPU's own timestamps
OctiMonitor.recordGPU(start: buffer.gpuStartTime, end: buffer.gpuEndTime, scene: currentScene)

// After the frame is committed
OctiMonitor.recordFrame(interval: now - lastFrame, cpu: CACurrentMediaTime() - now,
                        scene: currentScene, targetFPS: target, degraded: isReducedQuality)

Why GPU time per scene. Frame time tells you that something is slow. GPU time split by scene tells you where. In Opal Caress every material is a different shader, so it points straight at the shader to optimize.

Why the target rate is capped by the display. An app can ask for 120 fps on a phone whose screen only does 60. Judged against 120, every perfectly normal 16.7 ms frame would count as late. Capping by the screen's real maximum makes "late" mean late for this particular phone.

Why phys_footprint. It is the number iOS itself uses to decide when to kill an app for using too much memory, so it is the one that matters, not the virtual size.

Gaps of a second or more between frames are ignored. They mean the app was paused or in the background, not that a frame was slow.

GPU time is optional. An app without a Metal command buffer of its own can send frame timing alone; the dashboard then says that GPU time isn't reported instead of drawing empty bars.

Metrics and formulas

Frame budget

At 60 frames per second each frame has 1000 / 60 = 16.7 ms. Any work that doesn't fit makes the frame miss its slot on screen. The dashboard draws this budget as the dashed line on the charts and colours scene tiles by how much of it they use.

Dropped frame

dropped = interval > 1.5 × target interval

At 60 Hz the target interval is 16.7 ms, so any frame longer than 25 ms counts as dropped.

Why 1.5×, not 1×. Frame intervals naturally wobble by a millisecond or two around the target. A frame that misses its slot is shown one refresh later, so its interval is about 2× the target. The 1.5× line sits between the two: jitter is never counted, a missed slot always is.

Hitch time, ms per second

hitch time = Σ (interval − target) over dropped frames ÷ seconds played

For every dropped frame, how late it was beyond its own slot is added up, then divided by how long the app was in use. The result is milliseconds of lateness per second of use. This is the "hitch time ratio" Apple uses for scrolling and animation performance, and it is the main number on the dashboard.

Under 5 ms/sSmooth. People don't notice.
5 to 10 ms/sNoticeable. Occasional hitches.
Over 10 ms/sStuttering. People feel it.

Why hitch time instead of fps. Average fps hides stutter. An app can run at 58 fps on average while freezing for 200 ms every few seconds, and people feel those freezes far more than the average. Hitch time counts only the lateness people actually see, and it's comparable between 60 Hz and 120 Hz phones.

Frame rate

fps = frames drawn ÷ seconds played

Counted, not estimated from an average frame time, so pauses inside a session don't distort it.

Percentiles: p50, p95, p99

Within each 30-second window the frame times (and, separately, the GPU times) of a scene are sorted and read by the nearest-rank method: p95 is the value that 95% of frames are faster than. p50 is the typical frame, p95 is the slow end people run into regularly, p99 and max are rare spikes.

Why p95 and not the average. Smoothness is decided by the slow frames, and the average is pulled down by the many fast ones. p95 is the standard measure for "how bad does it regularly get" while still ignoring a single freak frame.

Reduced quality share

reduced quality = frames drawn in the fallback mode ÷ all frames

Shows how often the app had to lower its quality to keep up, which is a cost the frame numbers alone would hide.

The report

Every 30 seconds by default the app sends one row (Opal Caress uses 10 seconds in debug builds). If the phone is offline, the request times out or the server is busy (408, 429 or 5xx), up to 20 rows wait in memory and go out together with the next successful request; the oldest are dropped first. Any other 4xx means the server rejected the rows themselves, so they are dropped instead of blocking newer reports.

Why summaries, not raw frames. A minute of use is 3,600 to 7,200 frames. Sending them all would cost people data and battery. A 30-second summary is under a kilobyte and keeps everything the dashboard needs.

MetricKit data travels the same way with kind set to metrickit or diagnostic. It is stored for crash and hang investigation and isn't shown on the dashboard yet.

Privacy and security

  • No identifiers. No user, device or advertising IDs. The session id is random, made at each launch and never written to disk, so two launches of the same phone can't be linked.
  • Device model, not device. "iPhone15,2" says which kind of phone, not whose.
  • Insert-only key. The key inside the app can only add rows to the table. Row-level security blocks reading, changing or deleting.
  • Aggregates only. This page can read nothing but the output of one database function. It returns counts and averages, never a single row or session id.
  • HTTPS only, off by default. Without an https:// endpoint in the app's settings, reporting is off and the package never touches the network. The package ships its own privacy manifest.

Backend and aggregation

All rows land in one Postgres table, perf_reports, with the payload stored as JSON. The dashboard reads through octimonitor_overview(hours, app), which runs with the table owner's rights but returns only aggregated values.

How windows are combined

ValueCombined as
fpsAll frames ÷ all seconds
Hitch timeAverage of the windows, weighted by seconds played
Frame and GPU p95Average of the windows' p95, weighted by frame count
Dropped framesSum
Reduced qualityAverage of the windows' share, weighted by frame count
Active nowSessions that sent a report in the last 2 minutes

Why p95 is a weighted average. Percentiles can't be combined exactly from summaries: the p95 of a day isn't the average of the p95s of its windows. Getting it exactly would mean storing every frame. Weighting each window by its number of frames is the usual approximation, and it is accurate enough to spot trends, compare builds and rank scenes. If exact percentiles become necessary, the report can carry a small histogram instead.

Time buckets

For the over-time chart, reports are grouped into 1-minute buckets for the last hour, 10-minute buckets for 24 hours, and 1-hour buckets for 7 and 30 days. Each point is one bucket, so a range with a single bucket shows a single point.

Minutes into a session

For the heat chart each report is placed by how many minutes passed since that session's first report. "Running hot" is the share of time in those minutes whose worst heat state was serious or critical.

Health check rules

Simple, explainable rules, so every warning can be traced back to a number on the page.

FindingRuleLevel
StutterOverall hitch time over 10 ms/sCritical
Noticeable hitchesOverall hitch time from 5 to 10 ms/sWarning
Slow framesFrame p95 over 1.5 × 16.7 ms (25 ms)Warning
Misses the GPU budgetA scene's GPU p95 over 16.7 msCritical
Close to the budgetA scene's GPU p95 over 75% of 16.7 ms (12.5 ms)Warning
Slow on one phoneFrame p95 over 25 ms for a scene on one phone model, with more than 300 frames measuredWarning
Phones heat upIn some minute of a session, 30% or more of the time is serious or critical heatWarning
RegressionThe newest version's hitch time or frame p95 is more than 10% worse than the previous version'sWarning

Why 75% for GPU. The GPU isn't the only thing in a frame: the CPU, the compositor and the display all need their share. A scene that already takes three quarters of the frame on the GPU has almost no margin left on a slower or hotter phone.

Cost inside the app

A monitor that slows the app down measures itself. OctiMonitor is built to stay out of the way:

  • No memory allocation per frame. Frame and GPU times go into fixed-size ring buffers created up front. Recording a frame is a lock, one lookup and a few array writes.
  • Sorting happens rarely. Percentiles are calculated only when a report is built (every 30 s) or the debug overlay redraws, and the sort works in place.
  • System readings once a second. Memory and heat aren't read on every frame.
  • Bounded. At most 32 scenes are tracked separately; anything beyond goes into one "other" bucket, so a bug can't grow memory without limit.
  • Network off the main thread. One batched request per report, a 10-second timeout and no retries in a loop.

Known limits

  • Percentiles over long ranges are approximate, as explained above.
  • A window keeps the latest 4,096 frames per scene, about 34 seconds at 120 Hz, so the full 30-second window always fits.
  • Heat has four levels. iOS doesn't give apps a temperature, only nominal, fair, serious and critical.
  • One command buffer per frame is assumed for GPU time. A renderer that splits a frame into several buffers should add their times together.
  • Up to about 40 seconds of delay between a frame and the dashboard.
  • Debug builds are included and marked as such, so they can be filtered out or compared.
  • GPU time needs Metal. Apps that don't read it from a Metal command buffer report frame timing only.
