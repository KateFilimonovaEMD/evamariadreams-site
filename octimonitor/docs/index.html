The path of a number

Every value on the dashboard is measured on a real person's phone during normal use. Nothing is simulated or sampled in a lab.

  1. Measure. For every frame the app records when it actually reached the screen, how long the CPU spent preparing it and how long the GPU spent drawing it. Each frame is tagged with the app's current scene: a material, a level, a tool, whatever the app chooses.
  2. Summarize. Every 30 seconds, and whenever the app goes to the background, the frames of that window go into a small summary per scene: histograms of frame and GPU times, exact percentiles of the window, hitch time, dropped frames, memory and heat.
  3. Send. The summary goes out as one small JSON row to a Postgres table in Supabase. The app's key can only add rows, only for listed apps and only at a normal reporting rate.
  4. Aggregate. The dashboard calls one read-only database function every 10 seconds. It adds up the histograms of every report in view and reads the percentiles from the sum, so they cover all of those frames.

A frame drawn right now shows up on the dashboard within about 40 seconds: up to 30 seconds until its window closes, plus up to 10 seconds until the next refresh.

Scenes

OctiMonitor is not tied to one app or one kind of content. Every frame carries a scene: a short name the app chooses for what is on screen. All per-part numbers on the dashboard, such as GPU cost, the scene × device grid and the inspector, are split by it.

AppScene meansExample names
Opal CaressThe material being played withSlime, Spark, Lightning
A sculpting appThe active toolSculpt, Smooth, Paint
A gameThe level or screenMenu, Level 3, Boss fight

Several apps can report into the same backend. Each row carries the app's bundle id, so the dashboard can show all apps together or one at a time, each with its own scenes, phones and builds.

Why the app picks the name. Only the app knows which parts of it cost differently to draw. A fixed list would fit one app and be meaningless for the next. Use a small, stable set of names: they become the rows of every breakdown, and at most 32 are kept apart.

What the app measures

OctiMonitor is a small Swift package for any iOS app that draws with Metal. The app calls it in three places in its renderer; everything else happens inside the package. The example is how Opal Caress does it.

// When the GPU finishes the frame: the GPU's own timestamps
buffer.addCompletedHandler { b in
    OctiMonitor.recordGPU(start: b.gpuStartTime, end: b.gpuEndTime, scene: scene)
}
// When the frame actually appears on screen: the display's own timestamp
drawable.addPresentedHandler { d in
    OctiMonitor.recordPresented(time: d.presentedTime, scene: scene, targetFPS: target)
}
// After the frame is committed: CPU time and quality mode
OctiMonitor.recordFrame(interval: now - lastFrame, cpu: CACurrentMediaTime() - now,
                        scene: scene, targetFPS: target, degraded: isReducedQuality)

Why the moment of presentation. A frame can be drawn on time and still reach the screen late, for example when the system compositor is busy. People see presentation, and Apple's hitch metrics are based on it. Draw-call intervals are only the fallback.

Why GPU time per scene. Frame time tells you that something is slow. GPU time split by scene tells you where. In Opal Caress every material is a different shader, so it points straight at the shader to optimize.

Why the target rate is capped by the display. An app can ask for 120 fps on a phone whose screen only does 60. Judged against 120, every perfectly normal 16.7 ms frame would count as late. Capping by the screen's real maximum makes "late" mean late for this particular phone.

Why phys_footprint. It is the number iOS itself uses to decide when to close an app for using too much memory, so it is the one that matters, not the virtual size.

Gaps of a second or more between frames are ignored. They mean the app was paused or in the background, not that a frame was slow.

Metrics and formulas

Frame budget

Each frame has 1000 ÷ target rate milliseconds: 16.7 ms at 60 fps, 8.3 ms at 120 fps, 33.3 ms at 30 fps. Work that doesn't fit makes the frame miss its slot on screen. Every scene, phone and build is judged against the budget of its own target rate; when the scenes in view run at different rates, the GPU chart switches to showing each scene's share of its own frame.

Dropped frame

dropped = interval > 1.5 × target interval

At 60 Hz the target interval is 16.7 ms, so any frame longer than 25 ms counts as dropped.

Why 1.5×, not 1×. Frame intervals naturally wobble by a millisecond or two around the target. A frame that misses its slot is shown one refresh later, so its interval is about 2× the target. The 1.5× line sits between the two: jitter is never counted, a missed slot always is.

Hitch time, ms per second

hitch time = Σ (interval − target) over dropped frames ÷ seconds of use

For every dropped frame, how late it was beyond its own slot is added up, then divided by how long the app was in use. The result is milliseconds of lateness per second. This is the "hitch time ratio" Apple uses for scrolling and animation performance, and it is the main number on the dashboard. Because both parts are sums, it is exact for any range, app or phone.

Under 5 ms/sSmooth. People don't notice.
5 to 10 ms/sNoticeable. Occasional hitches.
Over 10 ms/sStuttering. People feel it.

Why hitch time instead of fps. Average fps hides stutter. An app can run at 58 fps on average while freezing for 200 ms every few seconds, and people feel those freezes far more than the average. Hitch time counts only the lateness people actually see, and it's comparable between 60 Hz and 120 Hz phones.

Frame rate

fps = frames shown ÷ seconds of use

Counted, not estimated from an average frame time, so pauses don't distort it.

Percentiles: p50, p95, p99

p95 is the value that 95% of frames are faster than: p50 is the typical frame, p95 the slow end people run into regularly, p99 and max are rare spikes. Two kinds are kept:

  • Within a 30-second window, the phone sorts that window's frames and reads exact percentiles (nearest rank). The HUD and the latest-reports table show these.
  • Over any range, percentiles come from histograms. The phone counts how many frames fell into each narrow time bucket; the server adds the counts of every report in view and reads the percentile from the total. That is the percentile of all of those frames, not an average of percentiles.
Bucket widthRangeLargest error
0.25 ms0 to 50 ms±0.125 ms
2 ms50 to 200 ms±1 ms
25 ms200 to 1000 ms±12.5 ms

Why histograms. Percentiles of separate windows can't be combined exactly: the p95 of a day is not the average of its windows' p95s, and the gap is largest exactly when it matters, when some windows have long freezes. Histograms add up without losing anything but the bucket width. It's how Prometheus and Datadog compute percentiles across many servers. Reports sent by older versions of the app have no histogram; any value that includes them is marked ≈ on the dashboard and falls back to a frame-weighted mean of window p95s.

Reduced quality share

reduced quality = draw calls in the fallback mode ÷ all draw calls

Shows how often the app had to lower its quality to keep up, which is a cost the frame numbers alone would hide.

The report

Every 30 seconds by default the app sends one row (Opal Caress uses 10 seconds in debug builds). If the phone is offline, the request times out or the server is busy (408, 429 or 5xx), up to 20 rows wait and go out with the next successful request; the oldest are dropped first. Any other 4xx means the server rejected the rows themselves, so they are dropped instead of blocking newer reports. When the app goes to the background, waiting rows are also saved to disk until the server confirms them, so closing the app while offline doesn't lose them. Each report says how many earlier ones were lost, and when its window ended, so late reports still land at the right time on the charts.

A histogram is a list of [bucket, count] pairs, only for buckets that have frames: usually a few dozen pairs per scene. Without GPU timing, gpu_ms is null and there is no gpu_hist.

Why summaries, not raw frames. A minute of use is 3,600 to 7,200 frames. Sending them all would cost people data and battery. A 30-second summary with histograms is one to two kilobytes and keeps everything the dashboard needs.

MetricKit data travels the same way with kind set to metrickit or diagnostic. It is stored for crash and hang investigation and isn't shown on the dashboard yet.

Privacy and security

  • No identifiers. No user, device or advertising IDs. The session id is random, made at each launch and never written to disk, so two launches of the same phone can't be linked by it.
  • Device model, not device. "iPhone14,7" says which kind of phone, not whose.
  • Insert only, within limits. The key inside the app can only add rows: row-level security blocks reading, changing or deleting. On top of that, the database accepts reports only for listed apps, only in the shape of a real report (sizes and values in plausible ranges), and at most 40 per session and 2,000 per app per minute.
  • What this page can read. Only the output of one database function: totals, merged histograms, and anonymous summaries of the 12 latest 30-second windows (time, phone model, app version, scene and its timings, without the session id). With very few phones in a range, such a summary can still describe a single phone's window.
  • HTTPS only, off by default. Without an https:// endpoint in the app's settings, reporting is off and the package never touches the network. The package ships its own privacy manifest.

What the limits can't do. A key inside an app can always be extracted, so the rules above stop floods and malformed data, not a determined person sending plausible fake reports. Proving that a report comes from a genuine copy of the app takes Apple's App Attest checked by a small server in front of the database; that is the next step for a commercial service, not for this demo.

Backend and aggregation

All rows land in one Postgres table, perf_reports, with the payload stored as JSON. The dashboard reads through octimonitor_overview(hours, app), which runs with the table owner's rights.

The function also returns how complete the data is: the share of frames timed at the screen, the share covered by histograms, the share with GPU timing, and reports lost on phones. The dashboard shows this as one line under the key numbers.

Time

Each report is placed at the moment its window ended on the phone (falling back to its arrival time if the phone's clock is implausible). The over-time chart uses 1-minute buckets for the last hour, 10-minute buckets for 24 hours, and 1-hour buckets for 7 and 30 days. Long ranges open on the most recent stretch; scrolling or pinching shows the rest.

Minutes into a session

For the heat chart each report is placed by minutes of use since that session started, from the phone's own clock, so a long session that began before the selected range isn't mistaken for a new one. "Sessions in serious heat" is the share of sessions that were serious or critical at some point in that minute. Older reports without the session clock fall back to the time since that session's first report in the range.

Health check rules

Simple, explainable rules, so every warning can be traced back to a number on the page. "Budget" always means the frame budget of the group's own target rate.

FindingRuleLevel
StutterOverall hitch time over 10 ms/sCritical
Noticeable hitchesOverall hitch time from 5 to 10 ms/sWarning
Slow framesFrame p95 over 1.5 × the budgetWarning
Misses the GPU budgetA scene's GPU p95 over its budgetCritical
Close to the budgetA scene's GPU p95 over 75% of its budgetWarning
Slow on one phoneFrame p95 over 1.5 × the budget for a scene on one phone model, with more than 300 frames measuredWarning
Phones heat upIn some minute of use, at least 30% of sessions (and at least 2) reach serious or critical heatWarning
RegressionThe newest build's hitch time or frame p95 is more than 10% worse than the previous build's, on the phone models both ran on, with at least 3 sessions and 5,000 frames on each sideWarning

Why 75% for GPU. The GPU isn't the only thing in a frame: the CPU, the compositor and the display all need their share. A scene that already takes three quarters of the frame on the GPU has almost no margin left on a slower or hotter phone.

Why matched phones for builds. If a new build happens to be used more on older phones, its averages get worse without any code change. Comparing the same phone models, each weighted the same for both builds, removes that effect.

Cost inside the app

A monitor that slows the app down measures itself. OctiMonitor is built to stay out of the way:

  • One to two microseconds per frame. A test that runs on every change records 200,000 frames (draw call, presentation and GPU time, as an app does) and prints the cost: 1.3 to 1.7 µs per frame on the CI simulator across runs, about 0.01% of a 16.7 ms frame. Real phones are typically faster than the CI machine; the test fails if it ever reaches 20 µs.
  • No memory allocation per frame. Frame and GPU times go into fixed-size rings and histograms created up front. Recording a frame is a lock, one lookup, a few array writes and two counter increments.
  • Sorting happens rarely. Window percentiles are calculated only when a report is built (every 30 s) or the debug overlay redraws, and the sort works in place.
  • System readings once a second. Memory and heat aren't read on every frame.
  • Bounded. At most 32 scenes are tracked separately; anything beyond goes into one "other" bucket. The disk queue holds at most 20 small reports for at most a day.
  • Network off the main thread. One batched request per report, a 10-second timeout and no retries in a loop.

To measure the cost on a specific phone, run the app in Instruments (Time Profiler and Allocations) with and without OctiMonitor.start() and compare CPU time per frame and allocations during the same scene.

How it's tested

Every change to OctiMonitor runs two test jobs on GitHub:

  • The Swift package is built and tested on the iOS Simulator: percentiles, histogram buckets and merging, frame pacing from presentation times, missing GPU timing, the retry policy and the cost per frame.
  • The database code runs against a real Postgres. A script simulates phones frame by frame, writes the reports the app would send, and checks the dashboard function's percentiles, fps, hitch time and heat shares against values computed directly from every frame. A second script, run as the public role, checks that unlisted apps, malformed reports, floods and reading the raw table are all refused, and that upgrading from the first version works.

Known limits

  • Percentiles are exact to half a bucket (0.125 ms below 50 ms). Values that include reports from older app versions are estimates and are marked ≈.
  • Screen timing needs the presented handler. Apps that don't register it are timed from draw calls, and the data-completeness line says so.
  • GPU time needs Metal. Apps that don't read it from a Metal command buffer report frame timing only.
  • One command buffer per frame is assumed for GPU time. A renderer that splits a frame into several buffers should add their times together.
  • Heat has four levels. iOS doesn't give apps a temperature, only nominal, fair, serious and critical.
  • Some reports can still be lost, for example when the app crashes while offline. Each report counts the ones lost before it, and the dashboard shows the total.
  • Reports can't prove they come from the real app. Limits stop floods and malformed data, not plausible fakes; see Privacy and security.
  • Up to about 40 seconds of delay between a frame and the dashboard.
  • Debug builds are included and marked as such, so they can be filtered out or compared.
